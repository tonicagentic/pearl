# Audience adaptation

Before writing, model the reader.

Do not adapt writing based only on demographic or occupational labels such as "executives," "developers," or "general audience."

Instead determine:

## 1. Prior knowledge

What can this reader reasonably be expected to know?

Do not explain concepts already inside that boundary.

Explain concepts immediately outside it when they become necessary to the argument.

## 2. Reader objective

Why is this person reading?

Determine whether they primarily want to:

* understand;
* evaluate;
* decide;
* implement;
* discover;
* be persuaded;
* or communicate the idea onward.

Optimize the writing around that job.

## 3. Starting beliefs

What does the reader probably already believe about the subject?

Identify where the argument agrees with, extends, qualifies, or contradicts those beliefs.

Spend explanatory effort where the required belief update is largest.

## 4. Likely questions

At each stage ask:

What would a smart, skeptical member of this audience ask next?

Whenever possible, answer that question before moving on.

This should determine the sequence of the argument.

## 5. Evidence threshold

Ask what kind of evidence this audience requires for each important claim.

Possible evidence includes:

* mechanism;
* measurement;
* example;
* benchmark;
* source;
* customer behavior;
* economic reasoning;
* historical evidence;
* mathematical argument.

Match evidence to the claim rather than adding evidence performatively.

## 6. Vocabulary

Use the most precise vocabulary the audience can reliably understand.

Technical language is useful when it compresses shared understanding.

It is harmful when it merely signals expertise.

Never replace a precise technical term with a longer vague explanation solely to sound accessible.

When a necessary term is unfamiliar, explain it once and then use it normally.

## 7. Abstraction level

Find the highest level of abstraction at which the reader can follow the reasoning without losing the underlying mechanism.

Move down an abstraction layer when the reader needs to understand why.

Move up when implementation detail no longer changes the conclusion.

## 8. Desired reader state

Before drafting, complete:

The reader currently understands/believes ______.

After reading, the reader should understand/believe ______.

The key realization connecting those states is ______.

Build the piece around that realization.

## Adaptation principle

Do not change the underlying truth for different audiences.

Change:

* what is assumed;
* what is explained;
* what is emphasized;
* what evidence is supplied;
* what examples are chosen;
* how quickly the argument moves.

Audience adaptation is primarily information selection and sequencing, not tone modification.

## Final test

Ask:

What can I safely assume?

Where is this reader most likely to get lost?

Where are they most likely to disagree?

What will they care about?

What must they understand for the conclusion to feel earned?

Then write for that reader.
