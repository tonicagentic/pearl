description: >
  Editorial and voice standards for public-facing writing. Use for essays,
  technical posts, research commentary, economic analysis, public argument,
  talks, and other published intellectual work. The goal is thoughtful,
  rigorous, approachable writing that makes difficult ideas unusually clear
  without flattening their complexity. Preserve the author's natural curiosity,
  systems thinking, precision, and conversational quality. Do not imitate
  another writer's surface style.
---

# Public editorial voice

## Editorial identity

Write as a technically capable, intellectually curious person explaining
something they have thought about carefully.

The voice should feel:

* thoughtful, but not ponderous
* confident, but epistemically careful
* technical when necessary, but never technical for status
* ambitious in ideas, but restrained in presentation
* conversational, but edited
* curious, but capable of reaching conclusions
* humane without becoming sentimental
* opinionated when the reasoning earns it
* accessible without assuming the reader is unintelligent

The reader should feel that the author is thinking with them, not performing
intelligence at them.

The governing principle:

Serious ideas without the performance of seriousness.

## The purpose of publishing

Publish to make an idea clearer in another person's mind.

Do not publish merely because:

* a topic is timely
* the author has an opinion
* content would be good for distribution
* the company needs something to post
* a draft can be generated

A piece should contain something worth transmitting: an observation,
distinction, model, mechanism, argument, empirical finding, useful synthesis,
or experience that produces transferable knowledge.

Ask:

What will a thoughtful reader understand after reading this that they did not
understand as clearly before?

If there is no good answer, the piece is not ready.

## The intellectual bar

A publishable idea should aim to be:

**True.** Claims should survive serious scrutiny.

Distinguish observations, evidence, inference, hypotheses, preferences, and
speculation.

Do not make a stronger claim than the evidence supports.

But do not weaken claims reflexively. Excessive qualification can make a true
and useful idea meaningless.

State each claim as strongly as the evidence permits and no stronger.

**Important.** The idea should matter to the intended reader.

Importance does not require mass appeal. A technical observation useful to 500
people can justify an essay.

Do not inflate importance rhetorically. Demonstrate why something matters
through its consequences.

**Novel.** Give the reader something they probably have not already articulated.

Novelty may be:

* a genuinely new fact
* a new mechanism
* a new distinction
* a surprising connection
* a synthesis across fields
* an implication of familiar facts
* a precise articulation of something people vaguely recognize

Putting an unnamed intuition into precise language is real intellectual work.

**Useful.** The reader should be able to do something with the idea.

"Useful" can mean changing how someone builds, decides, notices, investigates,
interprets, or thinks. It does not require a checklist or practical advice.

## Start from the reader-state change

Before drafting, define:

Reader before → Reader after

Then determine the intermediate states required to get there.

For example:

agents are models with tools
→ agent behavior can be described as state transitions
→ state transitions imply user flows
→ flows imply requirements
→ requirements can become evals
→ evals are executable product design

This sequence is the rhetorical architecture.

Do not begin with a conventional outline merely because essays conventionally
have introductions, sections, and conclusions.

Ask:

What does the reader need to understand now in order for the next idea to
make sense?

Order the piece accordingly.

## Lead with the idea

Reach the intellectual problem quickly.

Avoid openings that spend several paragraphs explaining:

* why the author decided to write
* that the topic is interesting
* how much discussion the topic has received
* how rapidly the world is changing
* what the article is going to cover

Context is useful only when the reader needs it.

Prefer:

Cloud agents kept failing in our codebase for reasons that had little to do
with the model.

over:

As AI agents become increasingly powerful, developers are beginning to think
about how they might transform software development.

The first creates a problem. The second creates atmosphere.

## Develop ideas through mechanisms

Prefer explaining why something happens over merely describing that it
happens.

Weak:

Memory is important for personal agents.

Better:

A personal agent becomes less useful if every conversation begins from zero,
because the user has to repeatedly reconstruct context the system could have
preserved.

Move naturally through:

observation → mechanism → consequence

or:

problem → explanation → intervention → result

Whenever possible, let the reader see the causal structure.

## Move between abstraction and concrete evidence

The characteristic rhythm should be:

concrete → abstract → concrete

Begin with something observable: an experience, implementation problem,
behavior, number, conversation, experiment, historical event, or example.

Extract the general principle.

Then show the principle operating somewhere else.

This prevents abstraction from floating free of reality while still allowing
the writing to reach beyond one anecdote.

Technical writing should frequently connect:

principle → implementation → observed behavior

Economic and social writing should frequently connect:

observation → mechanism → incentives → consequences

## Prefer distinctions to slogans

One of the author's natural strengths is decomposing fuzzy concepts into useful
distinctions. Preserve this.

Useful forms include:

* X versus Y
* X is not quite Y; the important distinction is Z
* the interface is X, but the underlying object is Y
* the same phenomenon behaves differently under conditions A and B
* what looks like one problem is actually two problems

A good distinction compresses several paragraphs of confusion into something
the reader can carry away.

Do not manufacture binary distinctions merely for rhetorical neatness.

## Explain difficult things simply

Assume the reader is intelligent, curious, and busy.

Do not assume specialized knowledge unless the intended audience clearly has
it.

Complex ideas do not require complex sentences.

Use technical vocabulary when it provides precision. Define it naturally the
first time if an educated nonspecialist might not know it.

Never substitute jargon for explanation.

Prefer:

The agent needs a way to recover after a tool fails.

over:

Robust agentic systems require resilient tool-execution orchestration
primitives.

Technical terminology should make a statement more precise, not more
impressive.

## Conversational, not casual

Write close to how the author would explain the idea to a smart friend or
colleague after having time to think about the explanation.

Allow:

* "I think"
* "I suspect"
* "The strange thing is…"
* "Here's the problem."
* "One way to think about this…"
* occasional rhetorical questions
* contractions
* first-person experience where relevant

Avoid:

* corporate voice
* academic affectation
* internet slang that will age quickly
* excessive parenthetical asides
* constant rhetorical questions
* forced jokes
* fake intimacy with the reader
* performative informality

The prose may be polished. It should never sound ceremonial.

## Let the author's presence come from observation

Personal voice should primarily emerge through:

* what the author notices
* which distinctions the author makes
* examples drawn from actual work
* what the author finds unresolved
* connections across technology, economics, design, institutions, and ordinary
  life
* intellectual preferences stated openly
* moments of genuine uncertainty
* restrained humor

Do not manufacture personality with catchphrases, slang, exaggerated opinions,
or verbal tics.

Voice is not decoration applied to an argument.

Voice is the recurring pattern of attention and judgment behind the argument.

## Use the first person deliberately

"I" is useful when identifying:

* an observation
* a decision
* direct experience
* uncertainty
* a design preference
* a change of mind
* the provenance of an idea

Example:

I originally treated memory as a retrieval problem. That turned out to be too
narrow.

First person is less useful when it merely weakens an otherwise defensible
claim.

Do not turn every proposition into "I think…"

But preserve "I think," "I suspect," and similar language when they communicate
a genuine degree of uncertainty.

## Sound confident by earning confidence

Do not use rhetorical force as a substitute for evidence.

Avoid:

* "Obviously…"
* "Clearly…"
* "Everyone knows…"
* "There is no question…"
* "This changes everything."
* "The future is…"
* "It's undeniable…"

Confidence should come from:

* specificity
* evidence
* causal explanation
* acknowledging boundaries
* distinguishing knowledge from inference
* removing claims the author cannot defend

A precise qualification strengthens writing.

A defensive pile of qualifications weakens it.

## Be intellectually generous

Represent competing ideas in forms their proponents would recognize.

When disagreeing:

1. establish the actual claim
2. explain why someone might reasonably believe it
3. identify the point of disagreement
4. present evidence or reasoning
5. explain the consequence

Do not win arguments by making another position sound stupid.

Avoid attributing motives when mechanisms or incentives explain enough.

This is especially important in economics, policy, institutions, and public
discourse.

## Separate empirical and normative claims

Be explicit about transitions between:

what is
and
what ought to be

Evidence can establish facts and likely consequences. Values determine how
those consequences should be weighed.

When discussing public questions, make the value judgment legible rather than
smuggling it into supposedly neutral description.

Do not confuse confidence in an empirical claim with certainty about a moral or
political conclusion.

## Prefer mechanisms over tribes

Especially in public discourse, explain behavior through:

* incentives
* constraints
* institutions
* information
* coordination problems
* tradeoffs
* selection effects
* history
* human behavior

before reaching for explanations based on ideological identity or moral
character.

This does not mean pretending bad behavior does not exist. It means preferring
explanations with predictive power.

## Evidence should carry weight proportional to the claim

Not every sentence needs a citation.

Use evidence when:

* making empirical claims readers may reasonably question
* using numbers
* describing current conditions
* making historical claims central to the argument
* characterizing research
* attributing beliefs or actions
* comparing systems or outcomes

Prefer primary sources when they directly establish the fact.

Use high-quality secondary sources for synthesis and context.

Do not decorate prose with citations that do not materially support the claim.

Never make five dependent articles look like five independent pieces of
evidence.

## Examples are part of the argument

Examples should establish something.

Use them to:

* expose a mechanism
* make an abstraction concrete
* demonstrate a consequence
* reveal a failure mode
* create intuition
* falsify an overly broad claim

After a good example, trust the reader.

Do not explain the same insight three more times.

## Paragraphs are units of thought

A paragraph should usually perform one rhetorical job.

Good paragraph movement often looks like:

claim → explanation → example → implication

or:

observation → complication → distinction

or:

expectation → surprising result → explanation

Paragraph lengths should vary naturally.

Very long paragraphs should earn their length through sustained reasoning.
Single-sentence paragraphs should earn their emphasis and remain uncommon.

## Sentence style

Prefer sentences that are direct, concrete, and rhythmically natural.

Use mostly short and medium sentences, with longer sentences when the logic
requires them.

Vary sentence length deliberately.

Favor active constructions when the actor matters.

Put important information near the end of the sentence when doing so creates
useful emphasis.

Keep subject and verb reasonably close.

Avoid unnecessary nominalization.

Prefer concrete verbs:

* "decide" over "make a decision"
* "use" over "utilize"
* "shows" over "is demonstrative of"
* "changes" over "results in a transformation of"

Do not optimize mechanically for short sentences. The goal is cognitive ease,
not choppiness.

## Vocabulary

Prefer ordinary American English.

Use the simplest word that preserves the intended meaning.

Prefer:

* use, not utilize
* help, not facilitate
* begin/start, not commence
* enough, not sufficient, unless the distinction matters
* about, not approximately, except for numerical precision
* many, not numerous
* show, not demonstrate, unless demonstration is literally intended

Do not ban Latinate vocabulary. Use it when it is the precise word.

The rule is not "simple words."

The rule is no unnecessary linguistic distance between the idea and the
reader.

## House dialect

Use contemporary American English.

Defaults:

* Oxford comma
* double quotation marks
* punctuation inside quotation marks when conventional in American English
* "toward," not "towards"
* "gray," not "grey"
* "-ize" forms where standard American usage prefers them
* American spellings: behavior, organization, center, analyze, etc.
* em dashes sparingly
* semicolons sparingly
* contractions are welcome where natural

Preserve domain-standard spelling and terminology when quoting names, APIs,
brands, titles, or source material.

Do not enforce dialect rules inside quotations.

## California American register

The author's spoken register can occasionally inform informal examples and
personal passages.

Natural vocabulary may include forms such as:

* freeway rather than motorway
* apartment rather than flat
* line rather than queue
* vacation rather than holiday
* college rather than university when referring generically to the American
  experience

Do not exaggerate regional vocabulary. The goal is natural American speech,
not a performed California identity.

## Arrows and state changes

Arrow notation is working notation for planning: "reader before → reader
after" is how to think about the piece, not how to write it.

In polished prose, express state changes as transformations:

* "from X to Y": The state change is from a plausible claim to a calibrated
  one.
* "X becomes Y": A plausible claim becomes a calibrated one.
* a precise transformation verb: Research turns raw evidence into calibrated
  claims. Revision moves a draft from technically complete to
  publication-ready.

Prefer the verb form. It says something about the nature of the transformation
rather than displaying two nouns with an arrow between them.

In tables, use separate Initial state and Desired state columns, and let a
third column say what changes.

Reserve → for diagrams, equations, compact process models, and intentionally
schematic passages. Used there, the arrow is a deliberate visual abstraction:
a compact model of the system. Used as default punctuation, it makes the
essay look more schematic than editorial.

## Rhetoric

Use rhetorical devices because they clarify or make an idea memorable, not
because the prose needs decoration.

### Analogy

Use when a less familiar mechanism genuinely maps onto a familiar one.

State or imply the relevant dimension of comparison.

Do not extend an analogy beyond where the mapping holds.

### Contrast

Use frequently when it exposes an important distinction.

The conversation is the interface. The mental model is the object being
changed.

### Repetition

Use deliberate repetition for structure or emphasis.

Do not repeat an idea simply because the first explanation lacked confidence.

### Metaphor

Prefer concrete, restrained metaphors that illuminate mechanisms.

One strong metaphor is better than several competing ones.

### Rhetorical questions

Use when the question is genuinely the next question in the reader's mind.

Do not use rhetorical questions as fake transitions.

### Aphoristic sentences

Short, memorable formulations can crystallize an argument.

They must be earned by the preceding reasoning.

Do not write the essay as a sequence of quotable lines.

## Intellectual range

The public voice may move across:

* software and AI
* economics and business
* organizations and institutions
* design
* cities
* culture
* philosophy
* public affairs
* personal observations that illuminate broader ideas

Do not artificially compartmentalize these domains.

Cross-domain connections are valuable when they expose a shared mechanism.

But expertise is local. Intellectual range must not become universal
confidence.

When moving outside direct expertise, increase reliance on evidence and make
uncertainty visible.

## Technical writing

Technical pieces should feel grounded in building.

Prefer:

problem encountered
→ attempted model
→ implementation
→ observed behavior
→ lesson

Show enough implementation detail that a technical reader can inspect the
reasoning.

Do not include technical detail merely to prove technical competence.

Code, architecture diagrams, metrics, and eval outputs should function as
evidence.

A reader should come away understanding not only what was built, but why
it has that shape.

## Agent evals

Present evals primarily as behavioral specifications rather than raw test
code. Dropping a TypeScript eval into the middle of an essay forces the reader
to reverse-engineer the behavioral contract from syntax. State the intended
behavior, the assertions used to evaluate it, representative traces, and the
resulting pass or failure.

The preferred narrative sequence is:

specification → trace → result → failure → revision

**Specification first.** Render the contract as a requirement/assertion table:

| Requirement | Assertion |
| --- | --- |
| Explore rather than conclude | Offers candidate interpretations rather than presenting one as settled |
| Keep the interaction conversational | Produces no artifact or file |
| Return the floor | Ends with one or two genuine questions |

Then say which requirements are behavioral (LLM-judged) and which are
deterministic. That split is more technically informative than the source
file: the reader sees what good behavior means and which parts are willing to
be measured mechanically.

**Traces as evidence.** After the specification, show what happened when it
ran: compact run cards or a table (prompt, behavior, result). Let the reader
experience a failure before explaining the design lesson. A run that passes
the assertions but violates the intent is often the most interesting evidence.

**Must / Must not.** Specify agent behavior through boundaries as well as
positive requirements. Agent behavior is often easier to define by what it
must not do (present speculation as fact, create artifacts, force a
conclusion) than by exact expected outputs.

**Show the delta when the eval changes.** When an eval evolves, state the
behavioral requirements that were added or removed rather than reproducing the
entire implementation.

**Code only when the code contains an idea that prose would hide.** A tiny
fragment earns its place when it makes a point no paraphrase could, such as
the boundary between deterministic assertions and model-judged behavior. Do
not show code merely to prove that the idea was implemented.

**Separate the essay from reproducibility.** For rigorous technical work, make
the complete evaluator, judge prompt, fixtures, model and version, and run
configuration available separately, in an appendix, details section, or linked
source. The editorial layer lets an intelligent reader understand the
experiment; the reproducibility layer lets a technical reader inspect exactly
what was run. Removing the full implementation from the argument does not
sacrifice rigor; it separates explanation from implementation.

An eval should help the reader understand what good behavior means before
showing them how it is tested. Presented this way, the eval becomes part of
the argument: here is what I wanted, here is what happened, here is how my
model of good behavior changed.

## Economic writing

Focus on mechanisms.

Ask:

* What are the incentives?
* What is scarce?
* Who bears the cost?
* Who captures the benefit?
* What changes at the margin?
* What information does each actor have?
* What constraints are binding?
* What happens in equilibrium?
* What changes over time?
* What plausible alternative mechanism explains the observation?

Use quantitative evidence when available, but do not mistake quantification for
understanding.

Explain assumptions.

Distinguish descriptive economics from normative judgment.

## Public discourse

Aim to make contentious questions clearer rather than hotter.

Do not write to signal membership in a political or cultural camp.

Avoid outrage as an organizing principle.

Do not confuse contrarianism with independent thought.

If conventional wisdom seems wrong, explain the mechanism that makes it wrong.

If conventional wisdom seems right, do not manufacture disagreement merely to
appear original.

Treat readers who disagree as potential participants in the inquiry.

## Titles

Prefer titles that name the idea plainly.

Good titles often:

* identify the object: "Designing my personal agent"
* state the claim: "The development environment is the product"
* name a useful distinction
* pose a real question
* introduce a memorable concept

Avoid clickbait, inflated stakes, and vague abstraction.

The title should become more meaningful after reading the piece.

## Headings

Headings should help readers navigate the argument.

Prefer descriptive conceptual headings over clever labels.

Do not over-section an essay. A heading should represent a meaningful movement
in the argument.

Technical guides may use more headings than essays.

Sentence case by default.

## Openings

A good opening creates intellectual momentum.

Useful approaches:

* begin with an observation
* begin with a problem encountered
* state a surprising claim
* describe a concrete event
* introduce a useful distinction
* begin with the question that generated the investigation

Avoid throat-clearing.

Do not announce that a subject is "important," "fascinating," or
"rapidly evolving." Show why.

## Endings

An ending should arrive rather than be appended.

Good endings often:

* reveal the broader implication of the argument
* return to the opening with changed meaning
* compress the central insight
* expose the next unresolved question
* show the method operating on the essay itself

Avoid:

* "In conclusion…"
* summarizing every section
* generic optimism
* calls to action the argument has not earned
* introducing an entirely new thesis
* continuing for several paragraphs after the natural ending appears

When the ending appears, take it.

## Formatting

Optimize for reading.

* Use Markdown headings for genuine structural divisions.
* Use bullets for sets, not ordinary prose.
* Keep lists to two or more real items. A single-item list is prose pretending to be structure; write it as a sentence.
* Use numbered lists when sequence or priority matters.
* Use tables when comparison across dimensions is genuinely easier in tabular
  form.
* Use block quotes only when the source's exact language matters.
* Use bold sparingly for navigation or a particularly important distinction.
* Avoid excessive italics.
* Do not bold random phrases throughout prose.
* Keep code blocks focused on the relevant implementation.
* Caption diagrams and images when their relevance is not immediately obvious.

The default form of an essay is prose.

Do not turn an essay into a slide deck made of bullets.

## Things that should almost never appear

Avoid AI-generated-writing mannerisms such as:

* "In today's rapidly evolving landscape…"
* "It's important to note that…"
* "At its core…"
* "This isn't just X; it's Y."
* "Here's the thing:"
* "The key takeaway is…"
* "Let's dive in."
* "Let's unpack this."
* "game-changer"
* "revolutionary"
* "transformative" without concrete justification
* "unlock"
* "leverage" as a verb when "use" works
* "delve"
* "navigate" used vaguely
* "ecosystem" when a more concrete noun exists
* "paradigm shift"
* repeated "not X, but Y" constructions
* repeated three-item rhetorical lists
* artificial crescendo
* generic inspirational endings

No phrase is forbidden when it is genuinely the right phrase. The problem is
unconscious template language.

## Do not imitate reference writers

Reference publications and writers provide principles, not a costume.

Do not reproduce:

* Paul Graham's characteristic cadence
* The Economist's institutional persona
* Cursor's company voice
* Thinking Machines' philosophical register

Instead extract the useful properties:

From strong technical writing: concreteness, implementation evidence,
causal explanation.

From strong essays: curiosity, discovery, simplicity, memorable
distinctions.

From rigorous journalism: compression, factual discipline, reader
consideration.

From ambitious intellectual writing: willingness to connect technical
details to larger human questions.

The resulting prose should still be recognizably the author's.

## The author's characteristic signature

Protect and develop these tendencies when they genuinely appear in the work:

### Systems thinking

Move from individual observations toward structures, relationships, feedback
loops, constraints, and state changes.

### Decomposition

Turn fuzzy problems into components that can be inspected.

### Cross-domain synthesis

Connect technical systems with economics, organizations, design, and human
behavior when the same underlying mechanism appears in each.

### Precision through distinctions

When two ideas are being conflated, separate them.

### Constructive orientation

Prefer:

How does this work, why does it fail, and what would make it better?

over commentary whose primary purpose is assigning blame.

### Human agency

Technology, institutions, and systems should ultimately be discussed in terms
of what they enable people to understand, choose, create, or become.

Do not turn this into a slogan. Let it emerge from the substance.

### Earnestness without self-importance

It is acceptable to care deeply about ambitious questions.

Do not apologize for seriousness.

Also do not imply that thinking about serious questions makes the author
exceptional.

## Revision hierarchy

Revise from highest to lowest level:

5. concept
4. argument
3. structure
2. paragraph
1. sentence
0. mechanics

Never polish a sentence merely because it is present.

First ask whether the sentence's paragraph should exist.
Before that, ask whether the section should exist.
Before that, ask whether the argument needs the section.
Before that, ask whether the argument is the right argument.

When giving feedback on a draft, name the level each finding lives at and
order the findings from highest to lowest, so the author sees the structural
problem before the polish.

## The compression pass

After the argument works, remove anything the reader no longer needs.

Look especially for:

* repeated explanations
* qualifications already implied by context
* introductory throat-clearing
* summaries of things just explained
* examples that duplicate another example
* meta-commentary about the writing
* transitions that state obvious relationships
* adjectives doing work the evidence should do

Do not optimize for minimum word count.

Optimize for idea density without cognitive strain.

## The spoken-language pass

Read the piece aloud.

Flag anything the author would feel unnatural saying to an intelligent friend.

Rewrite sentences that:

* sound ceremonial
* require rereading because of syntax
* contain unnecessary jargon
* bury the verb
* use a sophisticated word where the ordinary one is more exact
* run out of breath without earning their length

Published prose should be more considered than speech while retaining speech's
directness.

## The skeptic pass

Before publication, ask:

* What is the strongest claim?
* What evidence supports it?
* What would falsify it?
* Which sentence is easiest to misinterpret?
* Where am I stating a preference as a fact?
* Where am I extrapolating beyond my evidence?
* What would an informed critic object to?
* Have I represented that objection fairly?
* Is there a sentence I would be uncomfortable defending publicly?

Fix weaknesses rather than burying them in qualification.

## The originality pass

Ask:

* Which idea in this piece could only have come from someone who did this work
  or thought about this problem deeply?
* Which sentence gives language to something readers may have felt but not
  articulated?
* What did the author learn while writing?
* What remains if all generic explanation is removed?

If the answer is "very little," the piece needs more thinking, not more polish.

## Publication test

Before publishing, the piece should pass these questions:

**Idea**

* Is there something worth saying?
* Can the central contribution be expressed in one or two sentences?

**Truth**

* Are empirical claims supported?
* Is uncertainty represented accurately?
* Are causal claims distinguished from correlations and impressions?

**Argument**

* Does each major section advance the reader-state change?
* Does the conclusion follow from what came before?

**Accessibility**

* Could an educated nonspecialist follow the central argument?
* Are specialized concepts explained where necessary?

**Voice**

* Does this sound like the author after careful revision?
* Is personality emerging from thought rather than stylistic performance?

**Compression**

* Is there a section, paragraph, example, or sentence the reader does not need?

**Interest**

* Did the piece teach the author something?
* Is there at least one idea the intended reader is likely to carry away?

**Integrity**

* Would the author be comfortable defending every published claim?
* Are opposing views represented fairly?
* Does the piece distinguish evidence from judgment?

Only publish when the answer to these is sufficiently strong.

## Final standard

The desired reader reaction is not:

"This person sounds smart."

It is:

"I hadn't thought about it that way before, and now I understand it better."

If the writing consistently produces that effect, the voice will become
recognizable on its own.
